# The price test — one page, five conversations

Purpose: establish what an institution will pay per learner-month **without quoting a number you then
have to defend**. It is an interview instrument, not a survey, and it produces a decision rule rather
than a range.

Run it with 5 institutions before the pilot contract is drafted. Thirty minutes each, one interviewer,
notes in the customer's own words.

---

## The rules

1. **Never quote a price first.** The moment you say a number you have anchored the conversation and
   learned nothing. If they push, the answer is: *"I'll show you the pricing after I understand what it
   would be worth to you — otherwise I'd be guessing."*
2. **Never present more than three price points**, and never in a tier bundle at the same time. Price
   and tier are two tests; mixing them produces noise.
3. **Ask about decisions, not features.** "What would you do differently on Monday" beats "is this
   valuable".
4. **Record procurement thresholds.** In institutional buying, the number that matters is often not
   willingness to pay but *who can sign*. A price above the head teacher's threshold turns a purchase
   into a committee, and committee purchases die quietly.
5. **Do not discount to close a test.** A discounted yes is a no with better manners.

---

## Section A — what the problem costs them today (5 min)

Not "would you pay for AI". The existing spend, in their numbers:

| Ask | Why |
|---|---|
| What do you spend per learner per year on support for struggling learners? (tutoring hours, remedial staff, assessment, materials) | the budget line a purchase must displace or extend |
| How many learners leave or repeat a level each year, and what does that cost you? | the loss the product addresses, in their currency |
| How many hours a week does a teacher spend deciding what each learner needs next? | the labour being replaced |
| What did you spend on the last piece of software you bought for teaching, and how did you decide? | reveals the real buying process |

Write down the currency and the number. If they cannot produce one, that is a finding: no budget line
means no sale, regardless of price.

## Section B — the decision it would change (10 min)

Present the loop, not the technology: *the host app asks what this learner should do next; Adapt answers
from that learner's history; the answer always carries how sure it is, and sometimes it says "not enough
evidence yet".*

| Ask | What you are listening for |
|---|---|
| Walk me through what you do today when a learner is stuck. Where does that decision come from? | whether a decision is being made by guesswork, and by whom |
| If that decision were consistently better, what changes — marks, retention, teacher time, something else? | the outcome they will actually be measured on |
| Who notices if it works? Who notices if it fails? | the internal sponsor and the internal risk |
| What would have to be true in six weeks for you to renew without a meeting? | the renewal test, stated by them |

## Section C — the trade-off, before any number (5 min)

Three fixed choices, asked in this order:

1. **Per learner-month** — predictable, scales with your cohort, cheaper per learner as you grow.
2. **Per teacher or per site** — flat, capped, but you pay for learners who never log in.
3. **Per outcome** — you pay when a learner reaches a target; hardest to define, easiest to argue about.

Ask: *"Which of those three would your finance office actually sign, and why?"* Most institutions pick
option 1 or 2 for reasons that have nothing to do with price, and that answer decides your packaging
before it decides your number.

## Section D — the numbers (10 min, only now)

Show **three price points per learner-month**, no tier bundle, with this framing: *"these are three
possibilities we're testing, not an offer."* Use their currency. Typical anchors, adjusted to the
economy you're selling into: a low point near their existing per-learner support spend, a midpoint at
2–3×, a high point at 5×. Then four questions:

| Question | Codes |
|---|---|
| At which of these would you **sign without a meeting**? | the no-decision threshold |
| At which would you need **a committee**? | procurement friction |
| At which is it **too expensive to consider**? | ceiling |
| At which is it **so cheap you doubt it works**? | credibility floor — real, and usually the surprise |

Ask the fourth question explicitly. In education, an implausibly low price reads as a pilot that will be
abandoned, and it loses the sale as reliably as a high one.

## Section E — the renewal question (2 min)

*"If the first term went well, what would make you not renew?"* The answers are your risk register, and
they are free.

---

## Decision rule

With five conversations, per learner-month, in the local currency:

| Pattern | Read | Action |
|---|---|---|
| ≥3 say "sign without a meeting" at P, and 0 say "too expensive" at 2P | price is below value | set the bundle at **P × 1.5**, re-test once |
| ≥3 say "sign" at P, and ≥2 say "committee" at 2P | the threshold is the product, not the price | keep P, restructure into **annual** or **sub-bundle** to fit the signing authority |
| ≥3 say "committee" at P | the buyer is wrong, not the price | find the person who owns the budget line, re-test |
| Any say "too cheap to be credible" at P | packaging problem | move P up and add proof (model cards, coverage matrix), never discount |
| Nobody can name a current spend | no budget line | stop selling software; sell a **pilot with a measurement commitment** first |

Record every answer verbatim. When someone asks for a discount, the correct response is a smaller scope,
not a lower unit price — because a discount becomes the anchor for every renewal.

---

## What this is not

* Not a survey — five conversations, not fifty emails.
* Not a negotiation — you are not selling in these calls, and saying so up front gets you better data.
* Not an excuse to skip the pilot — willingness to pay is not willingness to use, and only the pilot
  tests the second one.
